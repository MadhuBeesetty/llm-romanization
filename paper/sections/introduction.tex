\section{Introduction}

Many speakers of South Asian languages communicate digitally in a native
language written with Latin characters. This practice is widespread but
non-standard: spelling varies across speakers, platforms, and contexts, and
English words are often mixed into otherwise Indic-language utterances.
Datasets such as Dakshina document the importance of Latin-script South Asian
language processing \cite{roark2020dakshina}, while Aksharantar demonstrates
the scale required for robust Indic transliteration systems
\cite{madhani2023aksharantar}.

Romanized generation presents a compound challenge for a general-purpose
language model. The model must preserve the source meaning, produce natural
target-language phrasing, and express that phrasing in a variable Latin-script
form. A model can fail at any stage by copying English, switching to native
script, omitting content, returning malformed structured output, or entering a
repetition loop. A semantic score calculated only over successful generations
can consequently overstate system quality.

We study this problem through a controlled English-to-Romanized Indic
generation experiment. A locally served Qwen3.5-35B model converts paired
English queries and responses into Romanized Hindi, Telugu, and Kannada. We
evaluate the outputs with multilingual BERTScore and BGE-M3 cosine similarity,
while separately tracking the number of scorable outputs.

Our preliminary contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item an end-to-end local pipeline for paired query--response romanization
    in three Indic languages;
    \item a comparison of token-alignment and embedding-based semantic metrics
    for English-to-romanized generation; and
    \item evidence that metric averages must be reported together with output
    coverage, because missing generations materially change interpretation.
\end{itemize}
