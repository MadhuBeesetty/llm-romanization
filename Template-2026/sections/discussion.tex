\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\noindent{\sffamily\bfseries Automated semantic evaluation can mask failures.}\enspace
BGE-M3 scores for the two strongest models (Anthropic, GPT-4o) cluster in the
0.73--0.84 range across all twelve model--language conditions. Considered in isolation,
this band suggests adequate semantic preservation. Our analysis reveals three
systematic failure modes that this band conceals. First, outputs that return
the English source verbatim---a complete task failure---receive BGE scores of
1.000, because the metric measures English-to-English similarity rather than
romanization quality. Thirty-five rows across Ollama Telugu and Kannada fall
into this category (16 and 19 respectively, matching the failure counts in Table~\ref{tab:failures}). Second, Gemini responses containing full paragraphs of
native Telugu or Kannada script receive BGE scores of 0.80--0.84---passing
marks for the wrong script entirely. Third, BGE and BERTScore disagree by
more than 0.15 points on 44\% of all 1,704 evaluated rows (maximum gap: 0.28
on a single response), indicating that the two metrics are not measuring the
same underlying property. When two standard metrics diverge this substantially,
neither can be trusted as a sole arbiter of quality.

\noindent{\sffamily\bfseries Model-specific failure modes.}\enspace
Gemini and Ollama fail in qualitatively different ways that reflect different
aspects of instruction following. Gemini produces \emph{script corruption}:
it generates otherwise fluent romanized text but switches to native script for
certain word classes, particularly abstract nouns and technical terms.
Frequency increases with script complexity (5.6\% for Hindi, 16.9\% for
Telugu). Ollama produces \emph{instruction collapse}: for Dravidian languages
it returns the English source unchanged, suggesting the model has insufficient
exposure to romanized Telugu and Kannada to complete the task. Crucially,
these failures do not co-occur: Gemini never copies English and Ollama never
leaks native script. The failure-aware scoring policy is essential for exposing
both, because silently excluding invalid rows would raise Gemini's Telugu BGE
from 0.630 to approximately 0.82---indistinguishable from GPT-4o's 0.794.

\noindent{\sffamily\bfseries Naturalness beyond embedding similarity.}\enspace
Independent analysis of the romanized outputs reveals a consistent register
problem that no automatic metric captures. All three API models generate text
that follows English syntactic structure with Indic words inserted at
corresponding positions---a pattern native speakers immediately identify as
translated rather than composed. Gemini Hindi consistently uses hyper-formal
Sanskrit-register vocabulary (\textit{saanskritik mahatva}, \textit{adhikar
hota hai}) that no speaker would produce in informal digital communication.
GPT-4o Kannada uses language-specific affirmatives (\textit{Haudu},
\textit{Khandita}) that signal greater lexical competence, but collapses into
the same translated-English architecture for expository prose. Among ollama's \emph{successful} Telugu outputs---those that are neither
verbatim English nor repetition loops---we observe authentic discourse particles
(\textit{raa}, \textit{Aa}, \textit{Aye}) absent from all other model--language
combinations, indicating real romanized-Telugu competence when the model engages
the task. That competence does not survive aggregation: 16 of ollama's Telugu
responses are verbatim English and several others degenerate, so its mean human
score is 4.07/12 (its worst language), while ollama's strongest language is in
fact Hindi (8.28/12). For the stronger models, the shared BGE range of
0.73--0.84 masks these divergences entirely.

\noindent{\sffamily\bfseries Query-type effects.}\enspace
Pooling scores across all models and languages, emotional and narrative queries
obtain significantly lower BGE scores (mean: 0.719) than technical and
scientific queries (mean: 0.816), a gap of 0.097 points (t-test $p < 0.0001$).
This is counterintuitive: one might expect code or mathematics to be harder
to romanize than conversational text. The explanation is that technical terms
have stable, widely-accepted romanizations (e.g., \textit{algorithm},
\textit{hybridization}) while emotional and idiomatic language resists
literal transliteration. Prompts requiring extended first-person narrative or
complex persona---such as roleplay and creative writing---are the hardest class
for all four models across all three languages. This finding suggests that
evaluation datasets for romanized generation should stratify by query type
rather than treating all prompts as equivalent.

\noindent{\sffamily\bfseries Output-token efficiency.}\enspace
Petrov et al.~\cite{petrov2023tokenization} showed that non-English \emph{input}
requires disproportionately more tokens under English-dominant tokenizers---a
cost asymmetry that disadvantages non-English users before the model runs. Our
data reveal a parallel asymmetry on the \emph{output} side. Table~\ref{tab:output-tokens}
reports the mean model-reported output tokens for each paired query--response
generation.

Anthropic reports the highest stable token usage, averaging 1,068 tokens,
compared with 714 for GPT-4o and 678 for Gemini. This difference should not be
interpreted directly as additional content or cost because each provider uses a
different tokenizer. GPT-4o is the most consistent across languages, while
Gemini reports the lowest overall mean. Qwen3.5-35B is substantially less
stable: its low Telugu mean is partly caused by incomplete or zero-token
outputs, whereas its Kannada mean is inflated by 49 generations that reached
the 4,096-token limit, frequently through repetition. Thus, mean token usage is
informative for the stable commercial systems but must be interpreted together
with completion and degeneration rates for Qwen.

\noindent{\sffamily\bfseries Implications for multilingual evaluation.}\enspace
Romanized Indic generation requires a metric bundle rather than any single
score: semantic preservation (BGE-M3 or BERTScore), coverage rate (fraction
of valid outputs), script compliance (native-character count), repetition rate,
and native-speaker acceptability. The failure-scoring policy must be stated
explicitly, because the standard practice of excluding failed outputs can
change model rankings entirely. More broadly, the results suggest that
embedding-based cross-lingual metrics are insufficient to evaluate generation
quality for low-resource romanized varieties---a population of hundreds of millions of users currently invisible to standard NLP benchmarks.
